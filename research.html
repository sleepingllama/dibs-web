01 · Definition

What researchers actually mean by “mental load.”

Mental load isn’t the chore itself. It’s the noticing, planning, and remembering that happens before the chore ever gets done. Interview-based research on roughly 160 people found that professional women carry high-level organizational thinking across every part of their life, work and home, while men typically compartmentalize that same planning skill to work only, deferring household planning to their partner once home, even when they’re skilled planners professionally.1

“Thinking about a household’s needs, long- and short-term, is taxing work, largely thankless, and often invisible to both the person carrying the mental laundry and their partner.”Allison Daminger, Harvard Business School

A 2025 typology paper gives this structure a useful shape: mental load sorts into “core” labor (recurring tasks like meal planning, that never really end) and “episodic” labor (one-off projects like planning a birthday party).2 That framing is part of why a standing household chore list — not a week-by-week schedule that resets every Monday — matches how people actually carry these responsibilities in their heads.

Even couples who describe their relationship as equal in every other way still tend to carry the invisible part unevenly: research across European countries found women do most of the anticipating and monitoring (noticing the milk is low, remembering the permission slip is due), while big, infrequent decisions are the part couples actually share.3 And carrying a disproportionate share of it is linked to measurable outcomes: elevated stress, reduced life and relationship satisfaction, and more work–family conflict.4

02 · The numbers

Who actually does more housework? The studies agree.

A UK survey of 4,010 heterosexual adults plus 250 LGBTQ+ adults found women report spending 36 hours a week on household tasks and life admin, versus 27 hours for men — a 9-hour gap, more than a full workday. Women are primarily responsible for 10 of 14 surveyed tasks; men for 4 (car maintenance, gardening).1 A separate 2,000-person US survey found much the same shape: 72% of cohabitating couples disagree on how chores should be handled, and partners spend an average of 2.6 extra hours a month on chores than their partner does.2

This isn’t a snapshot. It’s a pattern that holds. A 2025 systematic review of 38 studies spanning 2014–2024 confirms it consistently across the last decade of North American research: women, especially mothers partnered with men, continue to shoulder the majority of household labor compared to women in same-gender relationships or without children.3

72%of women say they do the majority of household tasks1
18%of their male partners agree that’s true1
9 hrsweekly gap between women’s and men’s household + admin time1

The gap shows up early and stays. Mothers carry more responsibility for 12 of 13 surveyed childcare tasks — mothers are 8x more likely to be the one dressing kids and 6x more likely to be the one changing diapers.1 None of this requires either partner to be doing anything wrong on purpose. It requires the work to become visible to both people, which is a solvable problem, not a character one.

03 · Conflict

Why couples actually argue about chores.

Household chores are a top-5 argument trigger for couples — cited by 21% of people, right behind tone/attitude, money, and communication style, and ahead of family relationships.1 The Gottman Institute lists housework among the six most common causes of marital conflict, and ties it directly to intimacy: unresolved chore conflict leaves “one partner feeling disrespected and unsupported, which leads to resentment.”2

“Women find a man’s willingness to do housework extremely erotic.”Dr. John Gottman, The Gottman Institute

The scale of it, measured directly: couples spend roughly 75 minutes a month specifically arguing about cleaning duties, and only 9% of partners consistently complete their assigned tasks without a reminder.3 Households where the work isn’t evenly shared argue about it 5 or more times a month; households where one person carries nearly everything argue 8 times a month.4 22% of the partner doing the majority of tasks report resenting their partner over it.4

75 minspent arguing about cleaning duties, per month3
8xa month couples argue when one partner carries it all4
9%of partners follow through without a reminder3

There’s a genuinely hopeful number buried in this research too: satisfaction with a partner’s contribution rises with relationship length — 56% satisfied under 6 months together, versus 76% satisfied after 10+ years.3 Who-does-what is a thing couples get better at. It’s learnable, not fixed.

04 · Perception

Does it matter who does more, or who agrees on it?

This is the finding the whole wheel is built around. A study of 33 couples found that for women, how their share of it felt to them was significantly linked to relationship uncertainty — but whether partners agreed with each other on who was actually doing what mattered more than the arrangement itself. Couples who agreed with each other, even about an uneven arrangement, had more stable relationships than couples who disagreed about what was actually happening.1

“Perceived fairness of these arrangements is often considered more important than the proportionality of individuals’ contributions.”PMC — Gendered Perceptions of Fairness in Housework and Shared Expenses

A larger study of 10,236 people found the same pattern at scale: only about half of people said their household’s arrangement felt right to them, and the strongest driver of dissatisfaction wasn’t feeling like their partner was shortchanged. It was feeling shortchanged themselves.2 A 2025/2026 study of 227 women in long-term relationships found household labor burden predicted lower relationship satisfaction regardless of partner’s gender, but having a say in who does what protected satisfaction, especially once kids were in the picture.3

“Having a say about who does what around the house is important to relationship health, especially when children are in the picture.”Nina Dours, study author

Put together, these studies point at the same mechanism: the fight usually isn’t really about the dishes. It’s about two people carrying different, invisible pictures of who’s doing what, and neither one can prove they’re right. Making the picture visible and shared, rather than negotiating it from memory, is the intervention the research actually supports.

05 · The bigger picture

Care work isn’t just a household issue. It’s a global one.

Zoom out far enough and this stops being a story about one kitchen. A UN Working Group formally concluded in June 2025 that failing care systems amount to a human-rights issue, not a private inconvenience:

“Fragmented and insufficient care and support policies amount to systemic gender discrimination,” affecting rights to education, health, employment, and political participation.UN Working Group, via OHCHR, June 2025

The scale is enormous. The International Labour Organization found that 708 million women worldwide are outside the paid labor force specifically because of unpaid care responsibilities — versus only 40 million men. For women aged 25–54, care duties are the reason two-thirds of them are out of the workforce.1 UN Women puts a dollar figure on the invisible half of that work: women and girls do 76% of unpaid care work worldwide, worth an estimated $10.8 trillion a year if it were paid.2

708Mwomen kept out of the paid workforce by unpaid care work1
$10.8Testimated annual value of unpaid care work globally2

None of that makes the fight about who left dishes in the sink feel any smaller in the moment. But it does mean the low-grade, unequal load so many households carry isn’t a personal failing. It’s a documented, worldwide pattern that researchers, economists, and now a UN body all describe the same way. Dibs starts at the kitchen sink because that’s where the pattern is small enough to actually fix.
